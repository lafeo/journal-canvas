# German Politics — Notes

Been following the German political situation closely. After the 2025 federal elections, the landscape has shifted considerably. Olaf Scholz's SPD took a real beating — the party lost ground in a way that surprised even pessimistic observers. Friedrich Merz and the CDU came out on top, but governing without a strong coalition is proving harder than the campaign rhetoric suggested.

What strikes me most is how immigration has become the defining fault line. The AfD's rise isn't just a protest vote anymore — it's crystallising around real anxiety about identity, jobs, and the pace of change. Merz has had to co-opt parts of that language to hold voters, which means the Overton window has shifted considerably rightward.

Berlin feels different from a year ago. There's a tension in the city that wasn't there before, or maybe I'm just noticing it more. Politicians in the Bundestag are debating everything from border controls to deportation frameworks in ways that felt unimaginable five years ago.

At the same time, Germany's economic challenges are structural and real. Deindustrialisation is accelerating — automotive, chemicals, manufacturing. The model that made Germany the export powerhouse of Europe is under strain from Chinese competition and energy costs post-Ukraine.

What Olaf Scholz got wrong: he underestimated how fast public sentiment moves when people feel economically squeezed. Friedrich Merz may have won the election but he inherited a mess that no rhetoric can fix quickly.

Brussels is watching nervously. A more nationalist German government changes the calculus for EU cohesion, especially on fiscal rules and migration compacts.

I keep coming back to this question: can Germany reform itself fast enough, or is it entering a long period of managed decline, patching things with short-term fixes while the structural problems compound?
