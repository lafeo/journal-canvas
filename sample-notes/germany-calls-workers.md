# Germany's Call to Skilled Workers

Germany is facing a demographic wall. The numbers are stark: by 2035, projections suggest Germany will be short roughly seven million workers across sectors — healthcare, construction, IT, engineering, care work. This isn't a future problem, it's already biting. Hospitals in rural Bavaria can't fill nursing positions. Construction projects stall because there aren't enough qualified tradespeople.

The response has been legislative. The Fachkräfteeinwanderungsgesetz — the Skilled Immigration Act — was significantly strengthened in 2023 and again updated in 2024. The changes made it meaningfully easier for qualified workers from outside the EU to get work visas, have foreign qualifications recognised, and ultimately pursue a path to permanent residence. Friedrich Merz has signalled he won't reverse this, even as his party takes a harder line on other migration issues. The economic necessity is too clear.

Olaf Scholz pushed hard for the skilled worker legislation during his chancellorship. It was one of the few areas of genuine cross-party consensus — the CDU didn't love it politically but couldn't argue against it economically. Berlin became particularly active in outreach, running recruitment campaigns in India, the Philippines, Brazil, and Vietnam.

The Indian pipeline is especially interesting. Germany and India signed a migration partnership agreement — the government in Berlin sees Indian engineers and IT professionals as a natural fit: English fluency, strong technical training, cultural adaptability. Munich and Berlin are the main landing cities. Munich for its industrial and tech ecosystem, Berlin for its startup culture and relatively affordable (by German city standards) housing. Frankfurt sees significant inflows of finance professionals.

There's a tension Germany hasn't fully resolved: it needs workers and is making legal pathways much clearer, while simultaneously the political mood around migration is hardening. The AfD frames any discussion of worker recruitment as contradicting border security. Friedrich Merz has to thread that needle constantly.

The climate context matters too — as weather disruption hits agricultural regions globally, migration pressure will increase regardless of any government's preference. Germany's skills shortage creates a channel for managed, mutually beneficial migration. Whether the politics allows that channel to stay open is the real question.
