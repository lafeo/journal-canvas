# Global Weather — What's Happening

The scale of what's happening with weather globally is still not fully registering in political discourse, even as the data becomes impossible to ignore.

Germany was having its hottest summer on record two years running now. The Rhine ran so low last year that cargo shipping was disrupted for weeks. Southern German farmers are starting to talk about crops they can no longer reliably grow — which is a sentence that would have sounded absurd a decade ago.

India is bearing an enormous share of the burden. The heat waves in 2024 and 2025 have been brutal — Delhi hit 47°C for multiple consecutive days, and parts of Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh were effectively uninhabitable in peak summer. The human cost is hard to quantify because a lot of heat deaths go uncounted, especially among daily wage workers and the elderly poor.

The monsoon has become less predictable too. Dr. Priya Sharma, a climate scientist whose work I've been reading, has documented how the monsoon's timing has shifted by two to three weeks over the past three decades. That disruption cascades through agriculture, water storage, flood risk — everything.

Australia's fire seasons now bleed into periods that were once safe. The Pacific Islands are watching their coastlines shrink. The Amazon is experiencing what ecologists call dieback — patches of rainforest converting to savanna because the internal rain cycle is breaking down.

The North Atlantic jet stream is behaving erratically, which explains why Germany gets both record heat and severe flooding in different seasons — weather systems that should track predictably are stalling or looping.

I think the concept I find most clarifying here is the idea of tipping points — not just linear warming but threshold effects where small additional forcing causes nonlinear jumps. Once enough permafrost melts, you release methane that accelerates warming further. Once the Amazon crosses a certain threshold, it can't self-recover. These aren't distant fears; the monitoring data suggests some of these systems are already in transition.

What's frustrating is that the political will needed to respond at scale still isn't there. Climate change remains something everyone acknowledges and very few governments actually prioritise above economic short-term pressures.
