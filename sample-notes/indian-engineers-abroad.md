# Indian Engineers — The Emigration Story

Something I've been sitting with for a while: India produces an extraordinary number of engineers — the IITs alone have an output that countries spend billions trying to replicate — and a disproportionate share of them leave within a few years of graduating.

The traditional story is they go to the US, Silicon Valley, the big tech companies. That's still true. But the newer story is more diffuse: Canada, Germany, Australia, the Netherlands, the UAE. The destination mix is changing as US visa pathways get clogged and politically contested.

Germany in particular has started appearing on the radar in a real way. Arjun Mehta, a software architect I know who moved from Bangalore to Munich two years ago, says the work-life balance is genuinely different and the path to permanent residency has become much clearer since the new immigration legislation. He's not alone — there are entire LinkedIn communities of Indians in Germany now sharing visa timelines and neighbourhood recommendations.

Dr. Priya Sharma has written about the paradox: India's investment in technical education is partly subsidising the growth of richer economies. The IITs are funded by Indian taxpayers; the value creation often happens elsewhere. She frames this as a form of structural capital transfer that no bilateral agreement adequately compensates for.

The reasons people leave aren't just economic, though salary differentials matter enormously — a senior engineer in Bangalore might earn 30-40% of what the same role pays in Munich or Berlin, even after adjusting for purchasing power. There's also the infrastructure frustration: power cuts, traffic, air quality in Delhi and Mumbai, the difficulty of getting things done in a system with significant bureaucratic friction.

What I find most interesting is the younger cohort — engineers who are 24, 25, they're not doing the classic "go to the US, get the H-1B" path. They're looking at Germany, looking at Canada, making more deliberate choices about where they want their career and their life to be. The brain drain conversation has a new texture now.

India's government knows this is a problem. There's no obvious solution though — you can't make people stay, and the conditions that make people want to leave are structural and slow to change. Better to build something in Munich than wait for Mumbai's infrastructure to become world-class.
